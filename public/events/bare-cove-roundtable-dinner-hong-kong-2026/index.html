Where AI Is Working in Hong Kong Asset Management: Our Roundtable Dinner With Bare Cove

We co-hosted a dinner in Hong Kong with Bare Cove Technology for technology, operations and data leaders from hedge funds and asset managers, to compare notes on where AI has reached production.

Date
October 27, 2026
Venue
JW Marriott Hotel Hong Kong, Admiralty
Format
Invite-only roundtable dinner
Hosts
Pascal AI Labs and Bare Cove Technology

About the Event

Hong Kong funds have spent the past year running AI pilots, and many are now deciding which ones to keep. We wanted to hear how those decisions are being made, so on October 27 we co-hosted a dinner at the JW Marriott Hotel in Admiralty with Bare Cove Technology, a technology provider to funds in the region.

Aditya Suresh, Managing Director at Macquarie Group, moderated a discussion with Rowel Balot, CTO of Bare Cove Technology, and Vibhav Viswanathan, our CEO and Co-Founder. The conversation then opened up to the whole room over dinner. The evening was held under the Chatham House Rule, so the points below are shared without attribution.

Six Things We Learned

  1. Most Pilots Stall Because They Lack Context

    Many pilots in the room had worked well in a demo and then stalled partway into the real workflow. The models could read and summarize. What they could not do was apply the fund's own knowledge: which company a ticker refers to, how the team builds a model, why a position was sized a certain way. The teams that got further added that context one workflow at a time.

  2. Staff Are Already Using Unapproved AI Tools

    Several firms said employees were using AI tools that IT had never approved, mostly for drafting and summarizing. The room saw this as a sign of demand. People use whatever answers them fastest, so an approved tool has to be at least as quick and as useful, or the unapproved ones will stay.

  3. The Model Is the Easiest Part to Replace

    Pricing, availability in Hong Kong and mainland China, and performance all change too often to commit to one model provider. Firms are moving toward setups where each step of a workflow can use whichever model suits it, and where swapping a model does not mean rebuilding everything else. The lasting cost sits in integration and in getting data ready.

  4. Funds Should Own Their Context and Rent the Model

    For a mid-sized fund with a lean IT team, building everything in-house is unrealistic. The part a fund should own is the layer that holds its definitions, corrections and the reasoning behind its positions. Contracts with AI providers should spell out training, data retention and ownership of outputs in detail, since a general promise to avoid training on client data is hard to enforce.

  5. Regional Separation Is a Design Decision

    Teams working across Hong Kong and mainland China need separate environments that respect mainland data export rules, with each person's permissions carried through from the source systems. One point won broad agreement: instructing a model to ignore a document is a policy, while making sure the model can never reach that document is a control. Regulators and investors ask for the control.

  6. Every Step Should Be Replayable for an Examiner

    Expectations from the SFC, the HKMA and investor due diligence questionnaires all point to the same requirement. For any AI step that touches client or NAV work, a firm should be able to show which documents the system read, which model it used and who approved the result.

Our Take

Each of these points comes back to where a firm's data, context and controls live, and whether the firm owns them and can audit them. That is the problem we work on: a context graph each firm owns, decision traces that keep the reasoning behind its work, and deployment inside the firm's own environment with existing permissions carried through, as described on our security page.

Thank you to Aditya, Rowel, the Bare Cove team and everyone who joined us.
